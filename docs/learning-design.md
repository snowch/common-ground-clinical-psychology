# Learning design

Audience: interested adult beginners who want to interpret human behaviour using evidence. The gap is between attaching an intuitive label and explaining a case with accurate concepts, alternatives and limits. Delivery is a standalone, accessible, learner-paced GitHub Pages course, not an LMS or certification.

The established café visual identity uses Libre Baskerville headings, Arimo body text, pale blue backgrounds, navy text, teal interactions and restrained chapter accents. Reading and controls precede the supporting figure in the stacked layout. The primary learning path remains clear when research disclosures stay closed.

Each chapter has a first decision, three separate narrated explanations, a visible evidence check, a new written transfer case, unaided retrieval and low-stakes field practice. The final case integrates chapters. Page visits indicate navigation, not mastery. No learner pilot or independent academic review has been conducted.

## 1. Understand distress without turning it into a label

Objective: Distinguish distress, impairment and diagnosis, and identify a proportionate support response.

Initial decision: What is the best first response to Sam?

Transfer: A person reports persistent difficulty that affects daily functioning, but no assessment has occurred. What should the course reader conclude?

Field application: Use a fictional scene to write an open question and one practical support option without naming a diagnosis.

## 2. Classification is a tool with limits

Objective: Explain what diagnostic categories organize and why dimensions and context still matter.

Initial decision: What explains different experiences within one diagnosis?

Transfer: Two trained clinicians independently assign the same classification from suitable information. What does the agreement establish most directly?

Field application: Rewrite a fictional diagnosis-as-identity statement to describe a person, a difficulty and an uncertainty respectfully.

## 3. Assessment gathers evidence; formulation connects it

Objective: Distinguish a screening score, clinical assessment and a revisable formulation.

Initial decision: What is the main limit of the questionnaire?

Transfer: A professional proposes a possible maintaining process after discussing a person’s history and current difficulties. What would strengthen the formulation?

Field application: Evaluate the stated purpose of a public screening tool without taking or interpreting anyone’s score. Identify one intended use and one limit.

## 4. Anxiety can protect and constrain

Objective: Explain how threat and avoidance may interact without diagnosing from ordinary worry.

Initial decision: What can Jo’s declined slot establish?

Transfer: A volunteer reports persistent anxiety affecting daily functioning and asks where to seek help. What should the team do?

Field application: Use a fictional presentation scene to distinguish a real hazard, ordinary anticipation and a possible maintaining process. Do not run exposure exercises.

## 5. Low mood has several possible contexts

Objective: Distinguish a depressive syndrome from one low mood and explain why assessment and support matter.

Initial decision: What is missing from the positive-attitude advice?

Transfer: A person hears that increasing activity is sometimes part of depression treatment. Which description of behavioural activation is accurate?

Field application: Use a fictional low-mood scene to identify one observation, one contextual barrier and one respectful support question.

## 6. Trauma exposure and diagnosis are different

Objective: Explain why trauma responses vary and why supportive care should not force disclosure.

Initial decision: What should change in Leo’s plan?

Transfer: A person becomes distressed near a reminder and does not want to discuss the past event. Which response is appropriate?

Field application: Review a fictional support response for safety, privacy and choice. Do not use personal trauma memories as an exercise.

## 7. Unusual experiences need careful assessment

Objective: Distinguish broad symptom domains from diagnoses and avoid stereotypes about danger or identity.

Initial decision: What is wrong with the headline?

Transfer: A person reports a distressing unusual experience and asks for help finding support. Which response is appropriate?

Field application: Rewrite a fictional stigmatizing headline using accurate, respectful language without diagnosing the person described.

## 8. Therapies make different kinds of claims

Objective: Compare treatment approaches and separate theoretical rationale from evidence for a specified use.

Initial decision: What question should lead the guide?

Transfer: A person feels treatment is not helping and wants to discuss progress with their clinician. What should the person be able to do?

Field application: Prepare three evidence-and-choice questions someone could ask a qualified provider, using a fictional care scenario.

## 9. Treatment claims need fair comparisons

Objective: Interpret average treatment effects using comparison groups, absolute outcomes, uncertainty and harms.

Initial decision: What is missing from doubled recovery?

Transfer: Using the invented rates, recovery rises from twenty to forty people per hundred. How should the invented result be expressed?

Field application: Use the invented teaching rates to write a balanced two-sentence result. Include one limitation and no treatment recommendation.

## 10. An informed next step is a useful outcome

Objective: Build a proportionate response that combines evidence, preferences, access and appropriate professional boundaries.

Initial decision: What should the information page offer?

Transfer: A fictional service user reports immediate danger and needs urgent help. What is the appropriate course response?

Field application: Draft an educational information card for a fictional service: scope, qualified care routes, privacy and an urgent-help distinction. Do not collect personal symptoms.
